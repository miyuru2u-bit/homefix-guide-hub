A home warranty can be arranged in connection with a property purchase, but it is separate from the home inspection, appraisal, homeowners insurance, title work, seller disclosures, repair negotiations, and lender requirements. The transaction documents should identify the provider, product, payment, and timing rather than referring only to “a home warranty.”

A plan paid through closing is not automatically active at closing, and a seller contribution does not prove that the coverage fits the buyer's property. Review the agreement before accepting it.

## Identify the product precisely

Record:

- Provider and administrator
- Exact plan name
- Property address
- Contract holder
- Covered systems and appliances
- Optional add-ons
- Service-fee selection
- Contract term
- Effective date
- Cancellation and transfer rules

If the purchase contract specifies only a contribution amount, determine who selects the plan and who pays any difference.

## Put the obligation in writing

The signed purchase agreement or amendment should state whether a warranty will be provided and who is responsible. Depending on the transaction, the buyer, seller, agent, broker, or another party may coordinate payment, but local practice varies.

Specify:

1. Provider or buyer-selection right
2. Minimum coverage or exact plan
3. Maximum contribution
4. Responsibility for upgrades and excess cost
5. Payment method
6. Deadline for ordering
7. Effective-date requirement
8. Remedy if the product is unavailable

Do not rely on a verbal promise near closing.

## Review the sample agreement early

Request the complete state-specific sample agreement while inspection and negotiation contingencies are still open when possible. A marketing brochure may not show component restrictions, limits, existing-condition rules, or claim procedures.

Check property eligibility, occupancy, covered items, quantities, exclusions, service fees, limits, contractor authorization, replacement method, cancellation, renewal, and transfer.

## Inspection and warranty serve different purposes

A home inspection reports observed property conditions within the inspector's scope. A home warranty is a service contract for listed items and eligible failures. Neither replaces the other.

Do not waive inspection or ignore a known defect because a warranty is being offered. A condition documented before coverage may be excluded or handled under different terms. Negotiate repairs, credits, or specialist evaluations based on qualified advice.

## Seller disclosures

A warranty does not remove disclosure obligations. Buyers should review seller disclosures, inspection findings, repair invoices, permits, and service history.

If an item is known to be failing, ask how it will be addressed in the transaction rather than assuming a future claim will pay for it. Legal duties vary, so consult qualified local professionals.

## Who pays at closing

The buyer, seller, or another agreed party may pay. The closing statement should reflect the transaction documents and local process.

Review the charge before signing. Confirm the amount, payee, contract holder, plan, and optional coverage. If the amount differs from the agreement, ask the closing professional to explain and correct it where appropriate.

## Escrow and closing documents

Do not assume every warranty payment is an escrow item. It may appear as a closing charge, seller-paid item, buyer-paid item, outside-closing payment, or separate invoice depending on the transaction.

Compare the purchase agreement, provider invoice, closing disclosure or settlement statement, and proof of payment. Ask the appropriate closing professional how the payment is handled.

## Effective date

Confirm the exact date and, if relevant, time coverage begins. A buyer product may begin at closing, after payment, after a waiting period, upon provider confirmation, or under another contract rule.

Ask what happens if closing is delayed, funds are not disbursed, the deed records later, or the order is submitted after closing. Obtain written activation confirmation.

## Waiting period

Real-estate plans can use timing rules different from direct consumer products, but do not assume every waiting period is waived. Check the issued agreement and declarations.

A timing exception does not necessarily make an already known or developing failure eligible. Existing-condition provisions can still apply.

## Seller or listing coverage

A seller may have listing coverage before closing. Determine whether that contract ends, transfers, converts, or is replaced when the sale completes.

Do not assume the buyer inherits the remaining listing plan. Confirm the buyer product, term, effective date, payment, and new contract holder.

## Buyer details

Verify the buyer's name, contact information, property address, and preferred delivery method. If the property is held by a trust, LLC, or another entity, confirm eligibility and authorized contacts.

Incorrect information can delay contract delivery or service requests.

## Covered items

Create a property inventory and verify each HVAC, plumbing, electrical, water-heating, kitchen, and laundry item. Additional units or specialty equipment may require add-ons.

Check components and causes—not just category names. A listed refrigerator, for example, may have exclusions affecting ice, water, sealed-system, door, or cosmetic parts.

## Coverage limits

Review per-item, component, event, annual, and aggregate limits. Ask whether diagnosis, labor, parts, tax, shipping, refrigerant, access, and prior claims count toward the maximum.

Compare meaningful limits with local repair and installed-replacement exposure for the property.

## Service fees and claim process

Confirm the service fee and when it is charged. Learn how to open a request, who selects the contractor, whether outside work can be authorized, and who pays diagnosis after denial.

The buyer should receive the contract number, claim contact information, and online-account instructions before or soon after closing.

## Local service availability

Verify service at the property address, particularly for rural locations, boilers, septic systems, wells, pools, built-in appliances, or specialty equipment.

A paid plan has limited practical value if qualified providers are unavailable. Ask what the contract permits in that situation.

## Repair and replacement

The administrator may choose repair, replacement, reimbursement, or cash settlement. Review valuation and whether delivery, installation, haul-away, utilities, trim, cabinets, permits, code work, and taxes are included.

Do not describe the plan to the buyer as complete replacement coverage unless the agreement supports that statement.

## Existing-condition language

Review known, unknown, pre-existing, and detectable-condition definitions. Keep inspection reports, disclosures, photographs, and repair records.

If coverage is disputed after closing, these documents can establish timing. They do not guarantee approval.

## Maintenance and installation

Check exclusions for maintenance, improper installation, modification, rust, corrosion, scale, sediment, pests, drainage, airflow, and unauthorized repair.

The buyer should receive available appliance manuals, maintenance records, and installation invoices from the seller where negotiated and available.

## Provider selection and referral relationships

An agent, broker, title company, buyer, or seller may help coordinate a plan. Ask whether any referral, marketing, or other relationship exists where disclosure is required.

Evaluate the contract independently. Another party paying does not remove the buyer's need to review coverage.

## Cancellation and renewal

Check cancellation timing, fees, claims deductions, refund method, automatic renewal, and notice requirements. Determine who receives any refund after a seller-paid plan.

Record the renewal date and likely responsibility after the initial term. A seller contribution does not imply future renewals are paid.

## Closing-day checklist

Before signing, confirm:

- The purchase agreement includes the warranty term
- The charge appears correctly
- The provider received the order and payment
- The buyer is the correct contract holder
- The plan and add-ons are correct
- The effective date is documented
- The final contract will be delivered
- Claim contact details are available
- Inspection and repair negotiations remain separate

## After closing

Save the final agreement, payment record, closing statement, inspection, disclosures, and provider confirmation. Register an online account where appropriate and verify contact information.

Create an inventory of covered equipment and keep model and serial numbers. Do not open a claim for a known pre-closing defect without reviewing how the transaction and contract address it.

## If the plan is missing

If the purchase documents require a plan but no contract arrives, contact the appropriate real-estate and closing professionals promptly. Compare the agreement, closing statement, invoice, and provider records.

Do not purchase duplicate coverage until responsibility is clarified.
