A major appliance just broke, a technician handed you a repair quote, and now you're stuck on the question every homeowner eventually faces: is it smarter to fix this thing or replace it? Make the wrong call and you either pour money into a dying machine or scrap one that had years of life left. The good news is that this decision follows a few reliable rules. Once you know them, you can confidently evaluate any appliance — refrigerator, washer, dryer, dishwasher, oven, or microwave — in a matter of minutes.

This guide gives you a repeatable framework built around three things: the cost of the repair, the age of the appliance, and its reliability history. We'll also cover the hidden costs and energy factors that people routinely overlook.

## The 50% rule

The most widely used guideline in the appliance world is refreshingly simple:

> If a repair costs **more than 50%** of the price of a comparable new appliance, replace it. If it costs less, repair it.

For example, if a new dishwasher costs $600 and your repair quote is $350, you're spending nearly 60% of replacement cost to fix an older machine — replacement is usually the smarter long-term move. But if that same repair is $150, fixing it is an easy call.

The 50% rule works because it captures a simple truth: the more you spend repairing an old appliance, the worse your return on that money, since the rest of the machine is also aging and may fail next. It's a quick gut-check, but it's most powerful when combined with the appliance's age.

## Average appliance lifespans

Age is the other half of the equation. An expensive repair on a near-new appliance may still be worth it; the same repair on a unit near the end of its life rarely is. Here's how long major appliances typically last with normal use.

| Appliance | Average lifespan | Lean toward replacing if older than |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Refrigerator | 10–13 years | ~10 years |
| Washing machine | 10–12 years | ~9 years |
| Dryer | 10–13 years | ~10 years |
| Dishwasher | 9–12 years | ~8 years |
| Oven / range | 13–15 years | ~12 years |
| Microwave | 7–10 years | ~7 years |
| Garbage disposal | 8–12 years | ~9 years |

A useful shortcut: once an appliance passes about **75% of its expected lifespan**, the odds of additional failures climb, and replacement starts to look better even for moderate repairs.

## The decision checklist

Run through these questions in order. The first "clear yes" usually settles it.

1. **Is it still under warranty?** If yes, repair it — often for free or a small fee.
2. **Is the repair under 50% of a new unit?** If no, lean replace.
3. **Is the appliance past 75% of its lifespan?** If yes, lean replace.
4. **Has it broken down repeatedly?** A pattern of failures signals the machine is wearing out — lean replace.
5. **Would a new model meaningfully cut energy or water bills?** If yes, factor those savings in — lean replace.
6. **Is this a minor, one-off fix on a newer, reliable unit?** If yes, repair.

> 💡 **Energy bonus:** Newer appliances can use 20–40% less electricity or water than models from 10+ years ago. Over several years, those savings can offset a meaningful share of a new unit's price — especially for refrigerators (which run 24/7) and washing machines.

## When repair almost always wins

Some situations point clearly toward fixing it:

- **Minor, inexpensive parts:** door seals/gaskets, hoses, belts, igniters, thermostats, drain pumps.
- **The appliance is under 5 years old** and has been reliable.
- **It's a premium or built-in model** with a long expected life and high replacement cost.
- **The fix is straightforward** and the part is readily available.

In these cases the machine has plenty of life left, and a modest repair restores years of service for a fraction of replacement cost.

## When replacement almost always wins

Other situations point clearly toward replacing it:

- **Compressor or sealed-system failure** on an older refrigerator.
- **Transmission, motor, or bearing failure** on an older washer.
- **Cracked tub, control-board failure, or multiple simultaneous issues.**
- **The repair quote approaches or exceeds half** the cost of a comparable new unit.
- **Parts are discontinued, back-ordered, or hard to source.**
- **The appliance has already needed several repairs** in the past couple of years.

These are the big-ticket failures that rarely justify investment in an aging machine.

## Don't forget the hidden costs

The sticker price of the repair isn't the whole story. Factor in:

- **Service call / diagnostic fees:** $75–$150 just to have the problem assessed, even if you decline the repair.
- **The risk of cascading repairs:** Fixing one worn part on an old machine often just shifts stress to the next weakest component.
- **Downtime and inconvenience:** A fridge or washer out of service for a week has real, if hard-to-quantify, costs.
- **Energy inefficiency:** An aging, struggling appliance can quietly inflate your utility bills month after month.
- **Disposal and installation:** When you do replace, budget for haul-away and installation of the new unit.

## A simple worked example

Say your 11-year-old washing machine needs a $400 repair, and a comparable new washer costs $700. The repair is 57% of replacement cost — already over the 50% threshold. The machine is also past its average lifespan, and a new model would trim your water and energy use. Three signals all point the same way: **replace.** Conversely, if that washer were only 3 years old and the repair were $150 on a $700 unit, every signal points to **repair.**

## How to find your appliance's age

If you don't know how old an appliance is, check the **serial number** on the data plate (often inside the door, on the back, or near the base). Most manufacturers encode the manufacture date in the serial number, and free online serial-number decoders can translate it for you. Knowing the true age sharpens every part of this decision.

## The bottom line

The repair-or-replace decision feels stressful in the moment, but it almost always comes down to three questions: How much is the repair relative to a new unit? How old is the appliance? And how reliable has it been? Apply the 50% rule, check the age against typical lifespans, weigh the hidden costs and energy savings, and the right answer usually becomes obvious. Use the checklist above the next time something breaks, and you'll make a confident, money-smart call every time.
